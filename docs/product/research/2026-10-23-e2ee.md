# End-to-end encryption of Flux content: evidence, 2026-10-23 (#615)

Research for [#615](https://github.com/ColdPhase/flux/issues/615) (founder request,
Hubert, 2026-10-18). The proposed contract built on it is [e2ee.md](../e2ee.md) and
the proposed decision row is F-030 in the [decision register](../decisions.md).
Author: claude-hubert (Claude Opus 5.5) in a supervised session. Nothing here is
an implementation, a prototype run or an external cryptographic review.

## Question

**Persona and situation.** An organization that self-hosts Flux behind its own
OIDC provider (Keycloak or another) wants a workspace whose conversations, tasks,
maps, Wiki and files cannot be read by whoever obtains the database, the files
volume or a backup: a hosting provider, a database administrator, a thief of a
backup disk. People still sign in with the usual OIDC account and expect their
phone and laptop to open Flux without typing a recovery code every day.

**Outcome.** Content is decrypted only on devices the person has approved; the
server stores and relays ciphertext; losing the database exposes no content.

**Uncertainties that could change the recommendation.**

1. Whether an audited, browser-ready group key protocol (MLS) is mature enough to
   carry persistent shared documents, or whether a simpler signed key-list design
   fits Flux better.
2. Whether browsers, including Safari and Home Screen web apps on iOS, can hold
   device keys safely and durably enough for a "trusted device" experience.
3. How much of Flux's current server behavior (search, notifications, F-021 live
   editing, F-022/F-027 AI, MCP, export) depends on reading plaintext.
4. Whether encrypted live co-editing with Yjs is proven in practice.

**Labels.** *standard* (RFC/W3C text), *vendor* (a maintainer's or company's own
description), *source* (code or package metadata inspected today), *observed*
(a run made for this record), *inference* (our conclusion), *unavailable*
(could not be read). All sources were accessed on 2026-10-23 unless stated.

## 1. Group key protocols

| Option | What the source establishes | Fit for Flux (inference) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **MLS, [RFC 9420](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9420.html)** (Standards Track, July 2023; *standard*) | "Efficient asynchronous group key establishment with forward secrecy (FS) and post-compromise security (PCS)" for two to thousands of members; costs "scale as the log of the group size". Assumes a largely untrusted Delivery Service but a **trusted Authentication Service** that binds identities to signing keys. Messages sent before a member was added "will not be accessible" to them. New members join by Welcome or external Commit. | Strong for chat. Flux's work objects are persistent and a newcomer to a project must read its history; MLS deliberately does not give that. A Flux design would still need a separate history-key mechanism, every device as an MLS leaf, a strictly ordered commit stream and per-device MLS state that can fork when a browser is offline or its storage is cleared. The AS role would fall to Flux's server unless clients verify identities themselves. |
| **Megolm/Olm** (Matrix; library [vodozemac](https://github.com/matrix-org/vodozemac)) | vodozemac 0.11.1 (2026-09-30, *source*), one audit by Least Authority (*vendor*, README). [Albrecht, Celi, Dowling, Jones, IEEE S&P 2023](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/485) found practically exploitable flaws that let **a malicious homeserver** read messages and impersonate users, from insecure design, protocol confusion and missing domain separation; Matrix shipped mitigations on 2022-09-28 (*standard*-grade peer review). In [November 2025](https://matrix.org/blog/2025/11/exclude-insecure-devices/) Matrix recommended, after MSC4153, not sending keys to devices that are not cross-signed, and Element announced excluding them by default from April 2026 (*vendor*). Matrix's 2024 "Invisible Cryptography" talk lists cross-signing, key backup and dehydrated devices as underused and hard to explain (*vendor*). | Lessons, not a library choice: (1) membership and device lists must be authenticated by users' keys, not asserted by the server; (2) require device signing from day one instead of retrofitting it; (3) per-sender ratchets and key requests produce "unable to decrypt" states and history gaps that are poor for persistent documents. |
| **Signal Sender Keys** (as deployed by WhatsApp; [WhatsApp Encryption Overview v9, 2026-02-25](https://www.whatsapp.com/security/WhatsApp-Security-Whitepaper.pdf)) | Each sender creates a chain key and signature key, sends them to each member over pairwise sessions, and "whenever a group member leaves, all group participants clear their Sender Key and start over" (*vendor*). Companion devices link by **scanning a QR code** that carries the new device's identity key and an ephemeral linking secret never sent to the server; the primary signs the companion into the account's device list (*vendor*). | The QR linking with a secret the server never sees, and the signed device list, transfer directly to Flux. Per-sender ratchets do not fit shared documents edited by many people. |
| **Signed key lists ("lockboxes")** (Keybase per-user and team keys; Anytype any-sync ACL; Bitwarden; Proton) | [Keybase](https://book.keybase.io/docs/teams/puk): a per-user secret seed encrypted to each device and paper key; revoking a device starts a new generation, encrypts the new seed to remaining devices and the old seed under the new one; team keys rotate "lazily (before the next write)" (*vendor*). any-sync's [`aclrecord.proto`](https://github.com/anyproto/any-sync/blob/main/commonspace/object/acl/aclrecordproto/protos/aclrecord.proto) has a space read key encrypted to each member's identity, invites, join requests and `AclReadKeyChange` carrying the new key for each remaining account (*source*, any-sync v0.13.8 tag). | Matches persistent, shared, history-readable content: one symmetric key per scope and epoch, wrapped to each member's public key, membership changes as a client-signed, hash-chained log, rotation on removal. Gives no forward secrecy for stored content, which no history-readable workspace can have. Uses only standard primitives (AEAD, HPKE, Ed25519, HKDF). |

**Disconfirming evidence for the lockbox choice.** It forgoes MLS's PCS: an
attacker who copies a current epoch key reads that epoch until the next rotation.
Matrix's experience shows that hand-built protocols accumulate design flaws; a
lockbox design is simpler than Megolm but is still Flux's composition and needs an
external review. MLS remains the standards-track answer for large dynamic groups.

| [hpke-js / @hpke/core](https://github.com/dajiaji/hpke-js) (RFC 9180) | @hpke/core 1.9.0; @hpke/dhkem-x25519 1.8.0 | `@hpke/core` ships a native Web Crypto `DhkemX25519HkdfSha256`; in its published `esm/src/kems/dhkemPrimitives/x25519.js`, `generateKeyPair` and every `importKey` pass `extractable: true`. `@hpke/dhkem-x25519` is implemented with `@noble/curves` (pure JavaScript over raw key bytes), not Web Crypto (packages unpacked and read 2026-10-23; correction from the independent review of `356b60f5`) | Passes RFC 9180 and Wycheproof vectors; "has not been formally audited." |
| libsodium / libsodium.js | libsodium-wrappers-sumo 0.8.4 (npm) | WASM/asm.js | libsodium 1.0.12–1.0.13 audited by Cryptography Engineering (Matthew Green) for PIA, [published August 2017](https://www.privateinternetaccess.com/blog/libsodium-audit-results/): no critical flaws (*vendor*). The JS build itself is not covered. |

**Inference.** No MLS implementation that runs in the browser has a published
independent audit today. The browser's own Web Crypto implementation (BoringSSL,
NSS, CommonCrypto underneath) is the most-reviewed code available to a web client;
it now covers every primitive a lockbox design needs except HPKE framing, which
`@hpke/core` composes from Web Crypto calls. Whether it can open with a recipient
key that Flux generated as non-extractable is untested (E0).

## 3. Account recovery and device models

| Product | Mechanism (all *vendor* pages) | Lesson for Flux |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [1Password Secret Key](https://support.1password.com/secret-key-security/) (page published 2026-10-06) | A 34-character Secret Key (~128 bits) combined with the account password; stored on signed-in devices and in the Emergency Kit; "We have no record of your Secret Key and can't recover it." | A printable kit with a high-entropy secret makes a stolen server database useless for offline guessing. |
| [Bitwarden SSO with trusted devices](https://bitwarden.com/help/about-trusted-devices/) | The client creates a device key that "never leaves the client" and a device key pair; the server stores the user key encrypted to the device public key and the device private key encrypted with the device key. New devices are approved from another device, by an administrator, or by master password. **"Account recovery is required to enable SSO with trusted devices"**: the user key is also encrypted to the organization's public key. | The closest product precedent for OIDC + trusted devices. Its administrator approval is key escrow: admins can grant themselves access. Flux must not make that the default if "IdP administrators must not decrypt" (#615 AC3) is to hold. |
| [Proton recovery](https://proton.me/support/recover-encrypted-messages-files) | A password reset does not restore encrypted data; recovery needs a device data backup (default on web), a recovery phrase, a recovery file or an old password; otherwise data may be permanently inaccessible. | Proton accepts "reset without recovery material = data loss" as its contract, and adds a trusted-device path to make it rare. |
| [Keybase](https://book.keybase.io/docs/teams/puk) | Device keys, paper keys equivalent to devices, per-user key generations on revocation, sigchain. | A recovery kit can be modeled as one more "device" of the account; revocation rotates the user generation. |
| [WhatsApp companion linking](https://www.whatsapp.com/security/WhatsApp-Security-Whitepaper.pdf) | QR code with identity key and a linking secret the server never sees; account signature over the device list. | Pairing that a malicious server cannot intercept. |
| Matrix | Cross-signing, secret storage with recovery key, key backup; moving to exclude non-cross-signed devices (see §1). | Make verification and backup part of the first-run path, not optional extras. |

**Identity provider.** [Keycloak Server Administration 26.8.1](https://www.keycloak.org/docs/latest/server_admin/index.html):
"Any user with the impersonation role in the realm can impersonate a user" and is
then "automatically logged in as the user being impersonated" (*vendor*). So an IdP
administrator, a password reset or an IdP compromise yields a valid Flux session.
**Inference:** no Flux key may be released to a session alone; a new device must be
approved by an existing device or by the recovery kit.

## 4. Encrypted collaborative editing

| Product | Observation | Lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Excalidraw](https://plus.excalidraw.com/blog/end-to-end-encryption) (2020-03-21, *vendor*) | A random 128-bit AES-GCM key in the URL fragment; the scene is encrypted with **a fixed all-zero IV**, safe only because each key encrypts one payload. | Link-as-key is simple but gives no membership or revocation; nonce discipline must be designed, not assumed. |
| [CryptPad developer guide](https://docs.cryptpad.org/en/dev_guide/general.html) (docs 2026.5.0; release 2026.10.0 on 2026-10-21, *source*) | Keys from the URL fragment; TweetNaCl xsalsa20-poly1305; editors sign messages with Ed25519 and invalid signatures are ignored; a "history keeper" stores the encrypted message log; accounts derive login keys with scrypt and "forgotten passwords cannot be recovered." The server sees channel ids, pin lists keyed by public keys and ciphertext. | Live co-editing over an encrypted append-only log works in a long-lived open-source product (*vendor*). Signing every update keeps a dumb relay from forging edits. |
| Proton Docs | Public client source [ProtonMail/WebClients](https://github.com/ProtonMail/WebClients) `packages/docs-core` and `docs-shared` depend on `yjs ^13.6.15`, `y-protocols` and `@lexical/yjs` 0.31.2 (*source*). Proton says keystrokes and cursor movements are end-to-end encrypted ([launch post](https://proton.me/blog/docs-proton-drive), *vendor*). | A large vendor ships encrypted Yjs editing in production. The internals of its envelope were not inspected for this record. |
| [secsync](https://github.com/nikgraf/secsync) | Encrypted snapshot, encrypted updates referencing it, encrypted ephemeral (cursor) messages; XChaCha20-Poly1305; Ed25519 signature on everything; per-client clocks per snapshot; the server persists an update only if the previous one was persisted; a new snapshot after a key rotation lets the relay drop old updates. States that it "doesn't hide meta data" and that the server can remove a user without detection. | The concrete envelope Flux needs for Yjs, minus key management, which Flux must supply. |
| Anytype any-sync | Encrypted DAG of changes per space; ACL list with read-key changes (see §1). | Same split: CRDT content plus a signed ACL. |
| Skiff | Encrypted docs and mail; announced acquisition by Notion on 2024-02-10 and sunset in 2024 (press, *vendor*-reported). | Users of an encrypted service need a working client-side export: no server can export for them. |

## 5. Browser platform

**Web Crypto.** [W3C Web Cryptography](https://www.w3.org/TR/webcrypto/) (First Public
Working Draft of the next level, 22 April 2025, *standard*) defines Ed25519 and X25519,
makes `CryptoKey` serializable so it can be stored in IndexedDB with its
`[[extractable]]` flag, and warns that it "does not guarantee that the underlying
cryptographic key material will not be persisted to disk, possibly unencrypted", that
users can clear storage "potentially destroying keys", and that user agents need not
zeroize key material.

Support from [MDN browser-compat-data](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data)
(`main` at 2026-10-22, *source*):

| Feature | Chrome | Firefox | Safari / iOS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ed25519 `generateKey`/`sign`/`verify` | 137 | 129 | 17 (randomized signatures, BCD note) |
| X25519 `generateKey`/`deriveBits` | 133 | 130 | 17 |
| `navigator.storage.persist()` | 55 | 57 | 15.2 |
| WebAuthn `prf` on `create` | 116 | 139 (135–138 partial, not macOS) | 18 |
| WebAuthn `prf` on `get` | 116 | 139 | **false** in BCD, with [WebKit bug 259934](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=259934) |

**PRF discrepancy.** WebKit's [Safari 18.0 post](https://webkit.org/blog/15865/webkit-features-in-safari-18-0/)
(2024-09-16, *vendor*) says Safari 18.0 "adds support for the WebAuthn `prf` extension"
to retrieve "a symmetric key from a passkey". BCD marks `get` unsupported and links bug
259934, "Implement PRF extension + hmac-secret", resolved fixed by commit 303406@main on
2025-11-21; a 2025-12-20 comment reports a crash for PRF with hardware keys in a
Technology Preview (*source*). Community tables (not authoritative) say iCloud Keychain
passkeys return PRF on iOS/macOS 18 while external security keys did not, and that
Windows Hello returned PRF only from a February 2026 Windows 11 update. **Inference:**
PRF can be an optional unlock factor; it cannot be the only way to unlock, and its
availability must be detected at run time (`getClientCapabilities`, a test assertion)
and verified on real authenticators. Docker WebKit emulation cannot exercise a platform
authenticator.

**Storage durability.** [WebKit storage policy](https://webkit.org/blog/14403/updates-to-storage-policy/)
(2023-08-10, *vendor*): best-effort storage can be evicted under pressure or after ITP
inactivity; `persist()` is granted by heuristics, for example when opened as a Home
Screen web app. [WebKit ITP, 2020-03-24](https://webkit.org/blog/10218/full-third-party-cookie-blocking-and-more/)
(*vendor*): script-writable storage, including IndexedDB and service-worker
registrations, is deleted "after seven days of Safari use without user interaction on
the site"; Home Screen web apps "have their own counter of days of use". Flux's
[mobile PWA contract](../mobile-pwa.md) already treats Home Screen and Safari tabs as
different install states. **Inference:** a Safari tab is an unreliable home for a
device key; a key loss there must be an expected, recoverable event (re-pair or
recovery kit), and the Home Screen web app should be enrolled as its own device.

## 6. Delivery of the client code

Web E2EE trusts the JavaScript that the server sends. [WEBCAT](https://freedom.press/tech/news/webcat-a-tamper-evident-seal-for-the-open-web/)
(Freedom of the Press Foundation with SecureDrop, *vendor*) checks single-page app code
against a signed, publicly logged manifest through a Firefox extension that is labeled
experimental/alpha. **Inference:** for a browser client, a malicious or compromised
operator who changes the served code can read content as users open it. E2EE in Flux
protects against theft of stored data (database, files, backups, snapshots) and against
a passive or read-only server compromise; it does not protect against an active
operator who ships modified code. A reproducible-build manifest and an optional
desktop wrapper can narrow this later; neither removes it for the plain web app.

## 7. Search, media and AI under E2EE

- **Server-side encrypted search.** Searchable-encryption schemes leak access and
  search patterns; leakage-abuse attacks against them are an established research line
  (Cash, Grubbs, Perry, Ristenpart, ACM CCS 2015, *standard*-grade literature, not
  re-read for this record). **Inference:** a server-side blind index would turn
  "nothing readable from the database" into "word frequencies and query patterns
  readable"; device-local search is the honest option.
- **Live media.** [LiveKit encryption](https://docs.livekit.io/home/client/tracks/encryption/)
  (*vendor*): E2EE for media and data tracks on self-hosted and cloud; its shared-key
  provider expects the application server to generate and distribute the key; "LiveKit
  does not (and cannot) store or transport encryption keys"; signaling is not end-to-end
  encrypted. Browser coverage, including Safari, is not on that page (*unavailable*).
  Flux's [media boundary record](../../development/live-media-encryption.md) states that
  today the SFU decrypts media. **Inference:** a Flux E2EE workspace must derive the
  media key on clients from the project key; a server-generated LiveKit key is not E2EE.
- **AI.** F-022 mode (a) `server` sends content from the worker to a provider; `runtime`
  runs a CLI in a server slot that reads through MCP; O-007/O-008 background compute
  and F-027 schedules, reactions and the morning summary read content in the worker.
  **Inference:** none can work when the server holds no keys. The founder direction of
  2026-10-23 keeps AI only inside the open app on the person's device. That needs the
  provider to accept requests from the browser: Anthropic enabled CORS for its API with
  the `anthropic-dangerous-direct-browser-access` header in August 2024 ([Simon Willison,
  2024-08-23](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Aug/23/anthropic-dangerous-direct-browser-access),
  secondary source; Anthropic's own documentation not re-read). Other providers'
  browser access is unchecked.

## 9. Measurement (*observed*)

`node:24-alpine` (Node 24.22.1), Web Crypto, one CPU of an AMD Ryzen 9 7900X, Docker
with no network, 2026-10-23; script kept outside the repository. Microseconds per
operation: HKDF object-key derivation 34.8; AES-256-GCM encrypt 64 B 28.4, decrypt
18.8; AES-256-GCM encrypt 1 MiB 1,070 (about 1 GB/s); Ed25519 sign 40.0, verify 84.4;
X25519 `deriveBits` 35.1.

**Inference.** Per keystroke batch the added cost is about 0.1 ms on a desktop CPU,
small next to F-021's p95 ≤200 ms gate; phones and browsers are unmeasured and likely
several times slower. Signature verification dominates cold loading: 10,000 unsnapshotted
updates would cost about 0.85 s of verification on this CPU, so clients must write
encrypted snapshots and verify the snapshot plus only the tail. A 64-byte Yjs update
gains a fixed header (version, ids, epoch, 12-byte nonce, 16-byte tag, 64-byte
signature: about 140 bytes), so updates should be batched per animation frame or
typing pause. None of this replaces the browser, phone and two-process measurements
required by #228/#231.

## 10. Inferences for Flux

1. Use a signed key-list design with standard primitives (Web Crypto AES-256-GCM,
   HKDF-SHA-256, Ed25519, X25519 with RFC 9180 HPKE); keep MLS as the reconsideration
   path when an audited browser implementation exists.
2. Keys are never derived from or released by the IdP. A device key is created on the
   device; a new device needs approval from a trusted device (QR or short code) or the
   recovery kit.
3. Device and membership lists are client-signed and hash-chained; the server orders
   and stores them but cannot add a reader whose key an authorized member did not bind.
4. E2EE removes server search, content-aware notifications, server AI, server export
   and server-validated live editing from encrypted workspaces; each needs a client
   replacement (device search, AI in the open app, browser export, client validation)
   or is unavailable. Some metadata, sizes and timing stay visible and must be listed.
5. The web delivery and the absence of an external audit are real limits that the
   product must state.
6. A signed key list is only as good as the binding of new members' keys and the
   detection of forks: invitations must bind keys (as any-sync's invite records do) and
   heads must travel in envelopes (review of `356b60f5`, P1-1 and P1-3).

## Gaps

- No prototype was built or run; #615 AC5 (Docker Keycloak, two people, several
  devices, Chromium/WebKit, plaintext inspection) remains open.
- Safari PRF on `get` conflicts between WebKit's 2024 post and BCD; real devices needed.
- Proton Docs' envelope, Skiff's whitepaper, Matrix's 2025 history-sharing work and
  LiveKit's browser matrix were not read in detail (*unavailable* for this record).
- No external cryptographic review exists; an agent review is not one. By founder
  direction (2026-10-23) independent agent reviews and tests are the accepted bar.
